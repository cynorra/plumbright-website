**Spread wood chip or shredded bark mulch 3 to 4 inches deep on well-drained soil and 2 to 3 inches on heavy clay, and keep it at least 6 inches away from tree trunks.** That's the guidance from Iowa State University Extension. Deeper than that keeps the soil too wet and cuts off air to the roots.

Finer mulches, such as shredded leaves or compost used as mulch, pack down more and should go on thinner than coarse wood chips.

## Why deeper isn't better

Mulch helps by holding moisture, keeping soil temperature even and blocking weed seeds from light. Past about 4 inches, the benefits flip:

- **Soggy soil.** A thick layer holds water against the soil surface, especially on clay.
- **Starved roots.** Roots need oxygen. Very deep mulch can push feeder roots up into the mulch itself, where they dry out in summer.
- **Rot and pests at the trunk.** Mulch piled against a trunk, the "mulch volcano," keeps bark wet. Iowa State warns that it can encourage cankers, root rot and rodents that chew the bark.

## Topping up an existing bed

Most beds don't need a full new layer every year. Old mulch breaks down slowly, so:

1. **Measure the layer.** Push a trowel or your finger down to the soil in a few spots and note the depth.
2. **Fluff it.** Rake the old mulch to break up any matted crust so water can get through.
3. **Add the difference.** If 1 1/2 inches remain and you want 3, add 1 1/2. The [mulch calculator](/mulch-calculator/) has an "already there" field that does this, and our [mulch quantity guide](/guides/how-much-mulch-do-i-need/) has charts by area.

## Edges and slopes

- **Pull mulch back from siding** a few inches so it doesn't hold moisture against the house or give insects a path.
- **On slopes,** shredded bark knits together better than chips and nuggets, which wash downhill.
- **Edging** keeps mulch from spreading onto lawn and paths and makes the depth easier to maintain.
