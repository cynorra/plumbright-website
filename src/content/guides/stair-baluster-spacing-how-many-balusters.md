**To find how many balusters a rail section needs, divide the clear length between posts, minus your target opening, by the opening plus one baluster width, and round up: N = (L − C) ÷ (C + W). The balusters then split the rail into N + 1 equal openings.** A 72 in section with 1 1/4 in balusters and a 3 3/4 in target opening needs 14 balusters, with openings of 3.63 in.

## The 4-inch rule is about the gap, not the center spacing

The International Residential Code requires that guards do not allow a 4 in sphere to pass through any opening (IRC R312.1.3 in recent editions; some jurisdictions and older code editions differ, so confirm with your building department). The rule measures the **clear opening** between balusters, not the distance from center to center. Spacing balusters 4 in on center leaves openings of 4 in minus the baluster width, which is safe but wastes balusters. Spacing the openings at 4 in, by contrast, fails inspection once you account for the extra width the baluster itself adds to the center-to-center distance. Aim for a little under 4 in, such as 3 3/4 in, so small measuring and cutting errors do not push an opening over the limit.

On stairs, the balusters follow the slope of the rail, and the triangular gap between the bottom rail, a tread and a riser is allowed to be larger (the code permits a 6 in sphere there). Check your local code for the stair-specific limit before you buy.

## The formula

Let L be the clear length between the two posts (or post and wall), W the baluster width and C the largest opening you will accept, all in inches.

1. **Number of balusters:** N = (L − C) ÷ (C + W), rounded **up** to a whole number.
2. **Number of openings:** N + 1, because a baluster is not set against the post at either end.
3. **Actual clear opening:** A = (L − N × W) ÷ (N + 1). This is always at or below C because N was rounded up.
4. **On-center spacing:** S = A + W. Use it to mark the center of each baluster.

Rounding up matters: rounding down gives openings larger than C, which is how DIY railings end up over 4 in.

### Worked example: 72 in section, 1 1/4 in balusters

1. N = (72 − 3.75) ÷ (3.75 + 1.25) = 68.25 ÷ 5 = 13.65, rounded up to **14** balusters.
2. Openings: 14 + 1 = **15**.
3. A = (72 − 14 × 1.25) ÷ 15 = (72 − 17.5) ÷ 15 = **3.633 in**, under 3 3/4 in.
4. S = 3.633 + 1.25 = **4.883 in** on center.

## Worked project example: 7 ft 6 in railing

A deck stair railing section is 7 ft 6 in (90 in) long and uses 1 1/2 in balusters.

1. N = (90 − 3.75) ÷ (3.75 + 1.5) = 86.25 ÷ 5.25 = 16.43, rounded up to **17** balusters.
2. Openings: 17 + 1 = **18**.
3. A = (90 − 17 × 1.5) ÷ 18 = (90 − 25.5) ÷ 18 = **3.583 in**.
4. S = 3.583 + 1.5 = **5.083 in** on center.

Buy 17 balusters plus a few spares for cutting mistakes. Set the first baluster so its edge is A from the post, then step off S for each one after. Dry-fit a few before fastening.
