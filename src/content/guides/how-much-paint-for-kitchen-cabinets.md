**A medium kitchen, about 20 linear feet of base cabinets and 16 feet of upper cabinets, has roughly 197 sq ft of surface to paint once you count both sides of the doors, the face frames and the exposed ends. That takes about 1 gal of primer and 1 gal + 1 qt of cabinet paint for two coats.** Small kitchens often need just a gallon of paint; large ones, two or more.

## Estimating cabinet area by the linear foot

Measuring every door is tedious, so we use a simple model based on the length of your cabinet runs:

1. **Front face area** = run length × cabinet height. We use 34 1/2 in for base cabinets (without the countertop) and 30 in for standard uppers.
2. **Doors and drawer fronts** cover about 85% of that face. Both sides of doors get painted, so count them twice. Drawer fronts are painted on the front only, but the backs of doors make up for it.
3. **Face frames** are the remaining 15% or so of the front.
4. **Exposed end panels**: about 2 ft × 34 1/2 in per base end and 1 ft × 30 in per upper end.

The model ignores cabinet interiors, which are rarely painted. It's an estimate; if your doors are unusually large or small, measure them instead.

## Worked example: a medium kitchen

- Front face: 20 ft × 2.875 ft + 16 ft × 2.5 ft = **97.5 sq ft**
- Doors and drawer fronts, both sides: 97.5 × 0.85 × 2 = **165.8 sq ft**
- Face frames: 97.5 × 0.15 = **14.6 sq ft**
- Two base ends and two upper ends: **16.5 sq ft**
- Total: **197 sq ft**. Primer: 197 ÷ 300 = 0.66 gal. Paint: 197 × 2 ÷ 350 = 1.13 gal.

Three quarts cost about as much as a gallon, so the primer rounds to a gallon. For the paint, a gallon plus a quart is the minimum; many people buy two gallons to have touch-up paint for years of kitchen use.

## Choose the right products

- **Primer:** a bonding primer made for glossy or factory-finished surfaces. Many cabinets have a slick lacquer or thermofoil finish that ordinary primer won't grip. Shellac-based or stain-blocking primer stops wood tannins and old grease stains from bleeding through.
- **Paint:** a cabinet or trim enamel, often a waterborne alkyd or urethane-modified acrylic, in satin or semi-gloss. These level out brush marks and cure harder than wall paint.
- **Thin coats:** two thin topcoats look better and wear better than one heavy coat. Allow the full recoat time on the label; cabinet enamels can take days to fully cure.

## Prep takes longer than painting

Remove the doors and hardware, label everything, degrease thoroughly (kitchen grease ruins adhesion), scuff-sand, vacuum and wipe down, then prime. The paint quantity is small; the surface prep is what makes the finish last.
