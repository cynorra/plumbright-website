**Buy 10% extra tile for a straight or offset layout, 15% for a diagonal layout and 20% for herringbone.** Add a few more percent for small rooms, rooms with many corners, and large-format tile, where one bad cut costs a big piece.

## Why small rooms waste more

Waste comes from the edges, and small rooms are mostly edge. We counted, for a straight grid with 1/8 in joints, how many tile positions need a cut because they meet a wall:

In a small bathroom, most tiles touch a wall, so most of them get cut. Many cut-offs are too small to reuse. That's why a 10% allowance that works in an open kitchen can leave you short in a powder room, especially with large tiles. For rooms under about 50 sq ft, use 15% even for a straight layout.

## Why diagonal and herringbone waste more

In a straight layout, the piece you cut off at one wall can often finish the row on the opposite wall. On a diagonal, every tile along every wall is cut into a triangle, and the two halves only fit back together if your cuts are perfect and the room is square. Herringbone is worse: each edge piece is an odd shape, and planks have to be cut on both ends near walls.

## Other things that push waste up

- **Large-format tile.** A tile 24 in or longer leaves big offcuts that may not fit anywhere else, and a single cracked tile is a bigger share of the order.
- **Natural stone and handmade tile.** Some pieces will be chipped or off-color, and you'll want to sort them.
- **Obstacles.** Toilet flanges, floor vents, island bases and door jambs all add cuts.
- **Patterns that must line up**, like a border or a matched vein.

## How many spares to keep

After the job, keep at least a few whole tiles, or a full box if you can. Tile lines get discontinued, and replacing one cracked tile years later is easy only if you have a match from the same lot. Count those spares inside your waste allowance, not on top of it.
