**Most asphalt shingles, both 3-tab and architectural, come 3 bundles to a square, and a square covers 100 sq ft of roof.** Some heavier designer shingles are packed 4 or even 5 bundles to a square; GAF, for example, notes its Woodland line comes 4 bundles per square. The wrapper on each bundle states its coverage, and that's the number to trust.

## From the footprint to bundles

You rarely know the roof area directly. Measure the house footprint including overhangs, then multiply by the pitch multiplier (see the [pitch multiplier chart](/guides/roof-pitch-multiplier-chart/)). Then:

1. **Squares** = roof area ÷ 100
2. **Add waste:** about 10% for a simple gable roof, 15% for hips, valleys and dormers. GAF suggests adding 10 to 15% for trim and waste.
3. **Bundles** = squares with waste × bundles per square, rounded up

### Example: a 6/12 gable roof

The footprint with overhangs is 44 × 30 ft.

- Roof area: 44 × 30 × 1.118 = **1,476 sq ft**, or 14.76 squares.
- With 10% waste: 16.23 squares.
- Bundles at 3 per square: 16.23 × 3 = 48.7 → **49 bundles**.
- Ridge: 44 ft of ridge cap. Starter: 155 ft along eaves and rakes.

## Why some shingles take more bundles

A bundle is sized so one person can carry it up a ladder. Heavier shingles, with thicker laminated layers or extra-large exposure, weigh more per square, so the manufacturer splits a square into 4 or 5 bundles to keep each one manageable. More bundles per square doesn't mean more roof coverage per dollar; compare price per square, not per bundle.

## Accessories that aren't in the bundle count

- **Starter strip** along the eaves (and often the rakes) gives the first course a sealed edge.
- **Hip and ridge cap** covers ridges and hips. Some people cut cap from 3-tab shingles; most architectural lines have a matching cap product, sold by the linear foot per bundle.
- **Underlayment** goes under the shingles; synthetic rolls often cover 10 squares.
- **Drip edge, flashing and vents**, plus roofing nails: four per shingle on a standard installation, more in high-wind areas, following the manufacturer's instructions.

## Fastener Specifications and High-Wind Nailing Codes

Proper nailing is what prevents entire squares of shingles from blowing off in high winds:

- **Nail Type and Length:** Use only **11- or 12-gauge hot-dipped galvanized barbed or ring-shank roofing nails** with a minimum 3/8-inch diameter head. Standard nail length is 1-1/4 inches for new decks; use 1-1/2 to 1-3/4 inches when installing hip and ridge caps or reroofing over existing layer. Never use staples or electro-galvanized drywall screws.
- **Nailing Pattern (4 vs. 6 Nails per Shingle):** For standard slopes up to 60 mph wind ratings, install **4 nails per architectural shingle** driven along the designated nail line. In high-wind coastal or mountain zones (IRC requirements for 110+ mph zones), building codes mandate **6 nails per shingle** and hand-sealing tabs with roofing cement.
- **Ice & Water Shield Barrier (IRC R905.1.2):** In regions where winter temperatures drop below freezing, local building codes mandate installing a self-adhering waterproof ice-and-water barrier along all eaves. The membrane must extend from the roof edge up the slope to a point at least **24 inches inside the interior heated wall line** to stop winter ice dams from backing water under shingles.

## Leftover bundles

Keep one or two unopened bundles for repairs after storms. Store them flat in a dry place. Shingle colors change between production runs and get discontinued, so a matching bundle years later is hard to find.
