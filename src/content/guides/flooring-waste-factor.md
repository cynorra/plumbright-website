**Use 10% for a straight plank layout in an ordinary room. Use 15% for small rooms, hallways and closets, rooms with many corners or doorways, and diagonal layouts; about 20% for herringbone. Reserve 5% for large, plain rectangular rooms laid by an experienced installer.**

## What cutting alone wastes

To see where the allowance goes, we modeled a straight layout row by row. Rules from typical installation instructions: end joints in neighboring rows at least 8 in apart, no piece shorter than 8 in, offcuts of 8 in or more reused to start a later row, and the last row ripped to width. The rooms are plain rectangles with no doorways or obstacles.

Two things stand out. In big rooms, the cuts themselves waste only a few percent, because nearly every offcut starts another row. In small rooms, waste jumps, because the room is short and narrow: every row ends in a cut, offcuts are often too short to reuse, and the ripped last row is a big share of the floor.

## So why not buy 3% extra?

Because the model is the best case. Real installations also lose material to:

- **Damaged planks.** Chipped tongues, cracked corners and off-shade boards get set aside.
- **Doorways and jambs.** Each doorway adds notches and undercuts, and often a short piece.
- **Out-of-square walls.** A room that's 1/2 in out of square over its length means tapered rips along one wall.
- **Mistakes.** A miscut long board can't be uncut.
- **Repairs later.** Keeping a few planks, ideally a full box, lets you replace a damaged board years from now with a perfect match.

Add those to the cutting waste and you land at the familiar 10% for ordinary rooms and 15% for small or busy ones.

## Ways to waste less

- **Plan the first and last rows.** Measure the room width and adjust the first row's rip so the last row isn't a thin sliver.
- **Start rows with offcuts.** The cut end of one row usually starts the next, as long as the joints stay staggered.
- **Lay out from the longest straight wall.**
- **Open several boxes at once** and mix planks, so color and pattern variation spreads evenly and you spot damaged pieces early.
